## 5. INSURANCE RESTORATION PAGE copy

### Section 1 — How wind damage actually happens (primer; 3D animation slot at top later)
Wind doesn't always rip shingles off. A strong gust gets under the tab, breaks the sealant bond, lifts
it, and lets it fall back with a crease or a hairline fracture. From the ground the roof looks fine —
but the seal is broken, water can get under, and the damage spreads. A few compromised shingles after
one storm can mean a much larger problem (and a much larger claim) than a homeowner realizes.

### Section 2 — The Connecticut Matching Law (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 38a-316e)

**Verified verbatim (source: Justia, 2024 Conn. Gen. Stat.; P.A. 13-138, S. 1):**

> **(a)** When a covered loss for real property requires the replacement of an item or items and the
> replacement item or items do not match adjacent items in quality, color or size, the insurer shall
> replace all such items with material of like kind and quality so as to conform to a reasonably
> uniform appearance. This provision shall apply to interior and exterior covered losses.
> **(b)** Nothing in this subsection shall be construed to impose liability on an insurer as a
> warrantor of any work performed pursuant to this subsection.
> **(c)** Nothing in this subsection shall be construed to authorize or preclude enforcement of policy
> provisions relating to settlement disputes.

**Plain-English summary (safe copy):**
Connecticut's "matching law" says that if a covered loss damages part of your roof and the new
shingles can't reasonably match the undamaged ones in quality, color, or size, your insurer is
required to replace enough to restore a *reasonably uniform appearance* — not just patch the damaged
section. In practice, that means a relatively small area of storm damage can entitle you to a far more
complete repair. (Connecticut's Supreme Court has also held that disputes over how much must be
replaced to achieve a uniform appearance are part of the "amount of loss" and go through the appraisal
process — 341 Conn. 735.)

**Compliance/tone note (do NOT remove):** Educate and motivate an inspection. Use language like
*"you may be entitled — let's find out."* Do **NOT** promise a guaranteed payout or a full roof;
matching outcomes are fact-specific and depend on the policy and the adjuster.
